## Are people still embracing loops?

**Yes, but less naively.** Every major harness shipped a goal loop within about six months. Google is consolidating its async agent into its loop-centric Antigravity, and Anthropic's Ultra plan drops the five-hour wall that long runs kept hitting. The June 2026 hype wave was followed by a July backlash, and what survived is a more modest practice: **bounded loops with hard checks**.

Two caveats keep this honest:

- **Enthusiasm isn't adoption.** The [August 2026 study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21884) mined 36,710 repositories and confirmed only a couple of hundred autonomous loop implementations (217 of 256 flagged, per a [summary](https://github.com/TianCai19/llmNote/pull/45)) <Evidence level="secondary" />, and found almost no committed loop state files. Loops are used, but mostly privately and without measurement.
- **The evidence is thin.** The same study found "no systematic evidence" for the claimed benefits; claims rest "almost entirely on anecdotes and self-reported productivity numbers."

## The problem map

| Domain | The fundamental issue | Read more |
|---|---|---|
| **Verification** | Self-reported success isn't success. Agents declare victory early or game the check. | [Verification](/guide/shortcomings/verification) |
| **Termination** | The loop doesn't know when it's genuinely finished, or that it has stopped making progress. | [Verification](/guide/shortcomings/verification), [Reliability](/guide/shortcomings/reliability) |
| **Cost** | Context is re-read on every turn, poll loops spin, idle wake-ups add up, and quotas run out. | [Cost](/guide/shortcomings/cost) |
| **Context and memory** | Long runs collect noise; compaction is lossy; handoffs between sessions are conventions, not standards. | [Context and memory](/guide/shortcomings/context-and-memory) |
| **Model choice** | Different steps need different capability and cost; cheap per token isn't cheap per task. | [Model choice](/guide/shortcomings/model-choice) |
| **Security** | Unattended runs, plus tools, plus untrusted input, plus persistent memory, give prompt injection room to act. | [Security](/guide/shortcomings/security) |
| **Humans** | Review capacity, comprehension debt, fatigue, and the effort of writing specs. | [The human bottleneck](/guide/shortcomings/humans) |
| **Reliability and observability** | Errors compound across iterations; operators can't see what happened or why. | [Reliability](/guide/shortcomings/reliability) |
| **Credibility** | Is "loop engineering" a discipline or a buzzword? | [The hype debate](/guide/shortcomings/the-hype-debate) |

## They're connected

These problems don't sit in neat boxes. Fixing one tends to move the bottleneck to another:

- A stronger **judge** reduces false "done" but adds **cost** per turn.
- A tighter **budget** controls cost but leaves more half-finished work for **humans** to review.
- **Fresh context** fixes context rot but adds orientation overhead and depends on good **state** files.
- More **autonomy** reduces human effort but enlarges the **security** blast radius.
- A cheap **model** lowers cost per token but may raise cost per task through retries, which hurts **reliability**.

<Callout title="If you only fix one thing">
  Make completion **checkable without trusting the agent**. A cheap loop that never finishes isn't efficient, and an
  expensive loop that falsely reports success is worse. Verification is upstream of almost everything else on this
  list.
</Callout>

## Three kinds of cost

It helps to split "cost" in three, because they have different fixes:

1. **Iteration cost.** The model calls themselves.
2. **Context cost.** Repeatedly re-supplying history, instructions, tool descriptions and state.
3. **Failure cost.** Iterations that produce no useful progress, plus recovering from mistakes.

A 30-iteration loop isn't necessarily more productive than a 5-iteration one. The third kind is the one that hurts most and is measured least.
